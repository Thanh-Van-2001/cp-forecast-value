Abstract

Large customers in ERCOT, PJM and other US markets pay transmission or capacity charges set by their load in a few system peak hours, and curtail on the days a forecast flags. Peak forecasts are sold on accuracy, but what accuracy, and the shape of a forecast's error, are worth in peaks caught has not been measured. Using a pre-specified plan and public EIA-930 data for four markets, 2018–2026 (94 peak hours), I compared six real day-ahead forecasts with five synthetic forecasts that have exactly the operator forecast's error but different shapes. More accurate real forecasts caught more peaks, but accuracy was not sufficient: at the operator's error, the shape of the error moved the share caught by up to 21 percentage points, from about 79% for random errors to nearly all for errors that keep the order of days (ex post selection of the budgeted days). The operators' free forecasts caught at least as many peaks as a weather-free model in every market. The public data need checking: confirming a recently reported offset, PJM's forecast is stamped one hour early relative to its demand, which official PJM peak hours corroborate. Realigned, the operator forecast caught 27 rather than 20 of 40 PJM peak hours. Peak forecasts should be judged by backtested capture at a stated number of alerts, on aligned data.
