Dear Editor,

Please consider the enclosed manuscript, "Accuracy is not enough: what decides whether a load forecast catches coincident peaks in four US markets", for publication in The Electricity Journal.

In ERCOT, PJM, NYISO and ISO-NE, large customers pay transmission or capacity charges set by their load in a few system peak hours. They curtail on the days a load forecast flags, and these forecasts are compared and sold on accuracy. At PJM's 2026/27 capacity price, a 10-MW customer that misses one of its five peaks pays roughly $240,000 more a year, before zonal scaling.

Verification theory says a forecast's value depends on the decision it serves. Studies of the value of load forecasts measure the utility's operating cost, and studies of coincident peaks take the forecast as given. None measures what accuracy, and the shape of a forecast's error, are worth in peaks caught at a stated number of alerts.

The manuscript fills this gap with a pre-specified study of public EIA-930 data, 2018 to 2026, 94 peak hours. Its contributions are:

1. A comparison of eleven forecasts under fixed, out-of-sample alert rules. Among real forecasts, accuracy and peaks caught moved together, so accuracy is necessary.
2. A same-error design showing that accuracy is not sufficient. At exactly the operator forecast's error, errors that keep the order of days caught nearly every peak and random errors about four in five (ex post selection of the budgeted days). The operator forecast itself sat 44 percent of the way from the random end to the order-keeping end.
3. A practical protocol for customers, vendors and regulators, with four questions to ask of any coincident-peak alert service. The operators' free forecasts are the baseline to beat.
4. A measurement of what a timing error in the public data does to such an evaluation. A one-hour offset in PJM's EIA-930 forecast was recently reported. Here it is confirmed against PJM's official peak hours, which show that the demand is correctly stamped. Uncorrected, it hides 7 of 40 PJM peak hours that the operator forecast catches.

Disclosure of related work. The PJM offset was first reported in an erratum to a public, non-peer-reviewed scorecard of EIA-930 forecasts (Guerra Filho, 2026, Zenodo). It was also found in a separate conference paper by the author on recording errors in EIA-930, currently under review at IEEE ISIE 2027 (ISIE27-000066). Both are cited. Neither examines coincident peaks. This manuscript shares no text with the conference paper, and its alignment check is an independent re-implementation on this study's data.

The manuscript reports two pre-specified hypotheses that were not supported and discloses five amendments to the analysis plan. Code, data-download scripts, the plan history and the results of every run are available at https://github.com/Thanh-Van-2001/cp-forecast-value.

The manuscript is original, has not been published elsewhere and is not under consideration by another journal. The use of generative AI tools is disclosed in the manuscript as the journal's policy requires. The author declares no competing interests and received no specific funding.
