Dear Editor,

Please consider the enclosed manuscript, "Which load forecast catches coincident peaks? Accuracy, the shape of the error, and a one-hour error in public data across four US markets", for publication in The Electricity Journal.

In ERCOT, PJM, NYISO and ISO-NE, large customers pay transmission or capacity charges set by their load in a few system peak hours. They curtail on the days a load forecast flags, and these forecasts are compared and sold on accuracy. Whether accuracy, or the shape of a forecast's error, decides how many coincident peaks a customer catches has not, to my knowledge, been measured. Work on coincident-peak response takes the forecast as given, and the closest prediction study uses one forecast per market without asking whether a better one would catch more.

The manuscript fills this gap with a pre-specified study of public EIA-930 data, 2018 to 2026, 94 peak hours. Its contributions are:

1. A comparison of eleven forecasts on the share of peaks caught under fixed, out-of-sample alert rules, including rules that hold the number of alerts equal. Accuracy separated poor forecasts from good ones but did not rank good ones.
2. A same-error design. At the operator forecast's exact error and equal alerts, errors that keep the order of days caught 97 to 100 percent of peaks and random errors about 80 percent. Forecasts that compress peaks failed when thresholds came from actual past peaks.
3. Evidence that neither accuracy nor a simple ranking score substitutes for a backtest of capture, and that the operators' free forecasts, bias-corrected, are the strongest baseline. Together these give customers, vendors and regulators a concrete evaluation protocol.
4. A measurement of how a timing error in the public data distorts such evaluations. PJM's day-ahead forecast in EIA-930 has been stamped one hour early relative to its demand since February 2019. Taken as published, it appears to catch 31 of 40 PJM peak hours at equal alerts; realigned, it catches 39.

The policy implications for customers, operators, EIA and regulators are set out in Section 5.

Disclosure of related work. The PJM offset was first noted in a separate conference paper by the author on recording errors in EIA-930, currently under review at IEEE ISIE 2027 (ISIE27-000066). That paper documents the offset as one of several data-quality issues. The present manuscript confirms it for the four markets studied and measures its consequences for coincident-peak capture, which the conference paper does not address. The two share no text, and the alignment check here is an independent re-implementation on this study's data. The manuscript reports two pre-specified hypotheses that were not supported and discloses five amendments to the analysis plan. Code, data-download scripts, the plan history and the results of every run are available at https://github.com/Thanh-Van-2001/cp-forecast-value.
