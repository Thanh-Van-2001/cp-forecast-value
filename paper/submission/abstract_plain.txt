Large electricity customers in several US markets pay transmission or capacity charges set by their load in a few system peak hours, and curtail on days a forecast flags as likely peaks. Such forecasts are sold on accuracy. This pre-specified study asks whether a more accurate day-ahead forecast captures more coincident peaks (CPs). It uses hourly demand and operator day-ahead forecasts (EIA-930) for ERCOT (4CP), PJM (5CP), NYISO and ISO-NE, 2018–2026 (94 CP hours), comparing six real forecasts with five synthetic ones that share the operator forecast's mean absolute error (MAE) but differ in the form of the error. Across all forecasts, MAE and capture were negatively rank-correlated (Kendall tau = −0.48 [−0.54, −0.41]), but the association came from separating poor forecasts from good ones: among the more accurate forecasts it was weak (tau = −0.11). At equal MAE the form of the error mattered: with the same number of alerts, errors that preserve the order of days captured 97–100% of CPs and noisy errors 79–80%. Peaks compressed towards the mean were harmless when alert thresholds were set on the forecast's own scale but captured 54% when thresholds came from actual past peaks. A score of how well a forecast ranks the season's top days was not a significantly better predictor of capture than MAE. Operator forecasts captured at least as many CPs as a weather-free machine-learning model in every market. CP forecasts should be compared by backtested capture at equal alert budgets, not by accuracy alone.
